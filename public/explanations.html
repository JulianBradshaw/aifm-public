1 Time horizon and the Automated Coder milestone

Stage 1 of our model primarily predicts the trajectory of AIs’ coding skills. We focus on coding automation due to coding being a strength of current AIs relative to other AI R&D tasks (and we focus on AI R&D tasks so that we can use them to predict how AI R&D automation will affect AI progress).

We extrapolate capabilities on the METR-HRS coding time horizon benchmark in order to predict coding automation. We choose METR-HRS due to the relative feasibility of extrapolating it to very advanced capabilities, and the years-long time period for which we have past data.

The coding time horizon, H⁢(t), is the length of the coding tasks the frontier model can successfully complete 80% of the time, as measured by how long it takes humans to complete them. For example, if an AI has a 2 hour 80% time horizon, that means it can complete with an 80% success rate tasks that take human baseliners 2 hours (and for tasks that take baseliners more time, it has a lower success rate). METR has found that time horizons have been growing exponentially over the last 5 years, with a doubling time of roughly 7 months. And if one only looks at the data since early 2024, it actually looks like time horizons are growing even faster, with a doubling time of 4-5 months.

We want to predict when the Automated Coder (AC) milestone is reached, that is, when we get a collective of AIs that fully automates an AGI project’s coding work.

Our model’s operationalization of AC.

An AC, if dropped into present day, would be as productive on its own as only human coders with no AIs. That is, you could remove all human coders from the AGI project and it would go as fast as if there were only human coders. The project can use 5% of their compute supply to run the AC.

A simple method for predicting the arrival date of an Automated Coder would be:

Simple Method for forecasting Automated Coder. Estimate what time horizon threshold (on an extended version of METR’s benchmark) would correspond to an Automated Coder, then use a straightforward exponential-in-time extrapolation of METR’s past data points to predict when this time horizon will be achieved.

Why an 80% success rate requirement?

80% is the highest success rate time horizon that METR considers high-quality. How is it possible that an 80% time horizon could correspond to dominating the average coder across all categories of tasks? (a) There is some randomness in whether an AI succeeds at an individual task, so even an AI that dominates average humans across all categories will not have 100% reliability at human-level or above efficiency on individual tasks. (b) We adjust the required 80% time horizon upward to account for increased reliability requirements.

Efficiency requirements for time horizons.

We explicitly impose both a speed requirement and a compute-efficiency requirement for an AI completing a task to count as success. Specifically, we require the AI to (a) complete the task as quickly as the median human at the AGI project who does similar tasks, and (b) use a proportion of present-day automation compute equal to the proportion of human coders working on the task at the AGI project. The METR paper does not set such speed and compute-efficiency thresholds, but we think this is important since AIs already sometimes accomplish tasks less efficiently than humans, and we expect this to become more important over time as AIs are better at translating more resources into better performance. These requirements should be roughly consistent with the METR data points to date.

Defining our theoretical benchmark METR-HRS-Extended.

Since METR-HRS only contains tasks that take human baseliners ≤ 30 hours to complete and we want to forecast up to much higher levels, we define a procedure that could in principle be used to extend the task suite even though it would not be practical to actually do so. We assume that the tasks used to extend METR-HRS are created using a similar process to the existing tasks: their task desiderata include aiming for tasks to be somewhat open-ended or requiring exploration to successfully complete, as well as covering a wide variety of skills and domains. They focus on text modalities and tasks that broadly play to models’ strengths, while still being economically valuable. We also assume that all tasks that are impossible to achieve reliably, for example because they are bugged or have ambiguous criteria, are filtered out.

It’s also important to define which human baseliners are used during the extension: the more competent they are, the larger an impact we should expect at any given time horizon. We assume that METR finds human baseliners who take the same amount of time to complete tasks as a typical AGI company programmer who does similar tasks. We think this might be loosely consistent with their past baselining, and this also represents a simple assumption that translates relatively well to real-world impacts.

However, the Simple Method misses out on some important factors that we want to take into account, namely:

  1. 1.

    The “time horizon vs calendar time” trend may be disrupted by changes in the growth of inputs to AI progress and the impact of AI automation. For example, the growth of training compute may slow due to limits on investment and the speed of building new fabs. Meanwhile, the human labor growth may slow over time (“there are only so many people you can usefully hire”), but the AIs getting better at coding and research will increase the aggregate labor working on AI R&D.

  2. 2.

    Time horizon may grow superexponentially or subexponentially in calendar time, even if input trends keep holding. Although METR has observed roughly exponential time horizon growth, with successive time horizon doublings taking roughly the same amount of time, the trend could become superexponential or subexponential (in fact the trend looks slightly superexponential at the moment because of the recent uptick, but we don’t actually consider this to be substantial evidence). Our best guess is that time horizon would grow superexponentially even absent AI R&D automation. This is because we expect AIs to reach infinite time horizons (at finite time), which requires superexponential growth at some point, and we don’t think there will be a sharp phase transition (more discussion below). On the other hand, one possible reason to expect subexponential growth, at least in the near-term, is data bottlenecks: it may be expensive to collect high-quality data for long-horizon tasks.

The next subsections explain the additions we make to the Simple Method (which already includes a present doubling time parameter τ and an Automated Coder time horizon requirement HAC) to address these shortcomings. Here’s a brief summary:

  1. 1.

    We track time horizon as a function of effective compute (which is a combination of training compute and software progress) rather than calendar time. In particular, to find the AC arrival time, the logic of our model is to first determine the effective compute EAC corresponding to AC (which we do by modeling time horizon vs effective compute and setting the AC time horizon HAC), then later find the AC arrival time by separately modeling the growth of effective compute.

  2. 2.

    We add a doubling difficulty growth factor d which determines whether (and the extent to which) time horizon grows superexponentially or subexponentially in log⁡(effective compute). Each time horizon doubling requires an increase in log(effective compute) that’s d times larger than the previous doubling. (Note that we’ve converted the question of time horizon growing (super/sub)exponentially with calendar time to the question of time horizon growing (super/sub)exponentially with log(effective compute).)

  3. 3.

    We add an optional effective compute “gap” that is required to reach AC despite the required time horizon having been reached. This represents the gap between succeeding on very long METR-style benchmark coding tasks and automating coding in the real world.

1.1 Replacing calendar time with effective compute

As mentioned above, we want to move away from directly thinking of time horizon as a function of calendar time, since calendar time trends aren’t mechanistic enough for our purposes and they may change in the future (e.g. because training compute growth slows due to investment limits, AIs helping with R&D introduces new dynamics, etc.). We chose to instead track time horizon (and other AI capabilities) as a function of effective compute, which is directly sensitive to changing inputs to AI progress. By definition, effective compute E⁢(t) is the amount of training compute we’d need to train models as performant as the frontier models at time t using the training process of the present-day. It’s a convenient metric that collapses software progress (i.e. more efficiently converting training compute into performance) onto the same axis as training compute. We’ll revisit the motivation and modeling of effective compute later, after we finish explaining our modeling of time horizons and the Automated Coder milestone.

1.2 Time horizon progression

Both the METR-HRS time horizon and effective compute increased roughly exponentially over the last 5 years. Therefore one might expect an exponential relationship between log(effective compute) and time horizon. We write τ for the number of orders of magnitude (OOMs) of effective compute needed to double time horizon. (Although we initially talked about time horizon growing exponentially as a function of time, the discussion translates to talking about time horizon as a function of (OOMs of) effective compute.) Intuitively:

effective compute increases by τ OOMs→assumes exponential1 doubling of time horizon 

We primarily set τ by fitting historical METR-HRS trends, balancing between the long-term observed trend and a potential recent uptick.

Now we address the second issue about the Simple Method we brought up, namely that the Simple Method assumes time horizon will keep growing exponentially as a function of log(effective compute). We model the possibility that time horizon may grow superexponentially or subexponentially via a parameter d>0, which we call the doubling difficulty growth factor. The idea is that each time horizon doubling should require d times what the last time horizon doubling took (in OOMs of effective compute). So d=1 corresponds to the exponential case, 0<d<1 to the superexponential case, and d>1 to the subexponential case.

Our best guess is that time horizon should grow superexponentially with log(effective compute), so that d<1. This is primarily because we expect that once AIs ultimately surpass humans at long-horizon agency, they will have infinite or extremely large time horizons (see the next expandable box). If an AI does have an infinite time horizon at a finite effective compute level, then (as the METR authors themselves point out) time horizon should grow faster than exponentially at least as one approaches that critical level of log(effective compute). While it is conceivable that growth would be roughly exponential up until we get close to that critical level of log(effective compute), our best guess is that there won’t be such a strong phase transition, and that the superexponentiality will become noticeable earlier.

Indeed, we have the intuition that increasing time horizon from, say, 1000 years to 2000 years should be easier than increasing time horizon from 1 hour to 2 hours; by the time an AI has a 1000 year time horizon, it presumably already has most of the long-horizon agency skills (long-term planning, decomposing long tasks, noticing and correcting mistakes, etc.) needed to get to the 2000 year time horizon. This intuitive argument also motivates our choice of the “next-doubling-is-d-times-easier” superexponential functional form we use in our model (a priori there are many choices for superexponentials). We are open to using a different superexponential functional form in our future modeling. See our supplementary materials for more discussion of superexponential time horizon growth.

When AIs develop better long-horizon agency skills than humans, they will likely have infinite time horizons.

Suppose we get AIs with better long-horizon agency skills than the METR-HRS-Extended human baseliners. If we plot the AIs’ success rate on tasks vs. the time it takes the human baseliners to complete those tasks (the “human-task-time”), what does that look like? There are three possibilities:

  1. 1.

    The success rate increases with human-task-time, reversing the trend shown by current AIs. This corresponds to an infinite 80% time horizon. Recall that our time horizon definition requires AIs to complete the task as quickly as the human baseliners and with an amount of automation compute proportional to the horizon length. Because of this requirement, an AI may have an “easier” time completing longer horizon tasks since it’s allotted more work time and compute to run on. In some sense the AI is better at long-horizon tasks than at short-horizon tasks, relative to humans’ skill profile.

  2. 2.

    The success rate decreases with human-task-time, but it asymptotes above 80%. This corresponds to an infinite 80% time horizon. The analysis is similar to item 1, but now the AIs are more often successful at short-horizon tasks than at long-horizon tasks (even if they’re allotted more work time and compute for the longer tasks). That said, they still beat humans at least 80% of the time at every task length.

  3. 3.

    The success rate drops below 80% but only for extremely long human-task-times. This corresponds to a finite (but extremely large) 80% time horizon.

We think (1) and (2) are substantially more likely than (3) for the human-relative conception of time horizons, though we still think (3) is a non-negligible possibility.

Achievement of infinite time horizon when time horizon growth is superexponential.

If 0<d<1, i.e. time horizon growth is superexponential in our model, we get an infinite time horizon when

log⁡E=log⁡E⁢(present-day)+τ1−d.

As discussed previously, an infinite time horizon implies that AIs are more competent at long-horizon agency than human baseliners, not that they have reached the limits of intelligence.

How do we set the doubling difficulty growth factor parameter d?

Eli’s median for the doubling difficulty growth factor is 0.92 based on intuitions about agency skills generalization and to a lesser extent by enforcing that the function backcasts previous time horizons reasonably, in particular those after GPT-3.5. Eli’s sampling distribution for d has 80% confidence interval [0.82,1.02]. See here for more discussion.

Explicit formula for time horizon including superexponential and subexponential.

Starting from H⁢(present-day), the first doubling of time horizon requires log⁡E to increase by τ, the second doubling by d⁢τ, the third doubling by d2⁢τ, and so forth. Provided H⁢(t) is not infinite yet (which eventually happens for d<1), we can calculate an explicit formula for H⁢(t):

H⁢(t)={H⁢(present-day)⋅2log⁡E⁢(t)−log⁡E⁢(present-day)τif d=1;H⁢(present-day)⋅(1−(1−d)⁢log⁡E⁢(t)−log⁡E⁢(present-day)τ)logd⁡2if d≠1.

1.3 Setting the effective compute corresponding to the Automated Coder

Now that we’ve defined how time horizon progresses as a function of effective compute, we need to translate this into the effective compute required to reach an Automated Coder.

We first set the time horizon HAC required to achieve AC. We set the AC time horizon requirement by estimating the time horizon of tasks that AI company programmers do, adjusting upward because our time horizons only require 80% success rate, then adjusting upward again for the difference between the time horizon benchmark and real-world coding tasks. See here for more discussion. Eli’s median (the default for the website) is 125 work-years and Daniel’s median is 3 work-years, so people have quite different intuitions. We invite you to try out your own values for this parameter.

Finally, we add an optional “effective compute gap” to our model to represent the possibility that the Automated Coder milestone may come later than when the AC time horizon requirement is achieved (e.g. maybe AIs will saturate METR-HRS-extended without fully replacing a human on real-world coding tasks, in a way that can’t be corrected for by adjusting the required time horizon).

2 Modeling effective compute

In our model, we take into account inputs including human labor, automated labor, training compute, experiment compute, and automation compute. We need some way to aggregate all these inputs together and map them onto concrete capabilties. Given that recent AI progress has been driven in substantial part by scaling up the compute used to train models, we decide to aggregate these inputs into effective compute: a metric that collapses software progress, i.e. more efficiently converting training compute into performance, onto the same axis as training compute. We then model concrete capabilities as functions of effective compute, as described in Automated Coder Effective Compute, Coding Automation, and Research Taste Automation.

More precisely, effective compute E⁢(t) is the amount of training compute we’d need to train models as performant as the frontier models at time t using the training process of the present-day. We express effective compute as the product of training compute Ctrain⁢(t) and software efficiency S⁢(t):

E⁢(t)=Ctrain⁢(t)⁢S⁢(t).

Software efficiency S⁢(t) measures how compute-efficient the training process at time t is. By definition, S⁢(Feb 2025)=1. And S⁢(June 2027)=1000 would mean: if we used the present-day training process, it would take 1000× more compute than in June 2027 to train models as performant as the best from June 2027. We use Feb 2025 because that’s when Grok 3 was released, and we are more confident about how much training compute was used for it than for other LLMs.

Limitations of using effective compute.

Effective compute is currently the best solution we know of for aggregating inputs to AI progress into a single performance metric. It tries to capture the past ten years’ scaling trends where more compute is converted into better performance. But, at least taken literally, it bakes in important assumptions, in particular that something like current AI training processes could scale to very high capabilities.

We think our model is still valuable even if this assumption fails, as one could make definitions of effective compute that more flexibly reference the training process of a fixed date (e.g. allowing some adjustments to the training process and different allocations of compute to better utilize large compute scale-ups), and this might lead to similar behavior. But it is certainly harder to interpret the effective compute values without this assumption and our model’s results may be off base because of this. In future model iterations, we may switch to using something like the Epoch Capabilities Index as our core capabilities metric, which may be more robust to paradigm shifts.

Another reason we might not want to worry much about the effective compute assumption (that scaling up compute would result in arbitrarily high capabilities) is that AI progress in our model comes mainly from software improvements, not increases in compute. So the case where the training process is kept fixed but compute is scaled massively doesn’t really come up. Given our actual model behavior, one might want to worry more about the assumption that you can get a lot of progress from software without more training compute.

Our model of the progression of software efficiency S⁢(t) depends on the variables of experiment throughput and (software) research effort. For clarity of exposition, we discuss these first and how they’re modeled. In Software Efficiency, we’ll explain how gains in research effort are converted into gains in software efficiency. Before all that, we say a bit more about the compute data our model uses.

2.1 Compute forecasts

In our model we track 3 types of compute:

  1. 1.

    Training compute Ctrain⁢(t), a direct input to effective compute.

  2. 2.

    Experiment compute Cxpm⁢(t), an input to software efficiency.

  3. 3.

    Automation compute Caut⁢(t), an input into coding automation.

These compute trends are modeled as exogenous time series, i.e. they’re not affected by what else is going on in the model. This means we aren’t modeling hardware R&D automation, hardware production automation, or economic growth, which is a serious limitation of our model, particularly in slow takeoff scenarios.

We forecast compute growth by first estimating how much compute the leading AI company has, then determining what percentage of their compute they are using for each of training, experiments, and automation. For the leading AI company’s compute, we project a ∼2x slowdown in its growth rate by 2030, and further slowdowns afterward. This is due to investment and fab capacity constraints. We refer the reader to our supplementary materials for more details on our compute forecasts.

2.2 Experiment throughput

Experiment throughput X⁢(t) represents the AI project’s capacity for implementing and running experiments. It can roughly be understood as the number of experiments the project manages to implement and run per unit time, where experiments are weighted by how much compute they use and how labor-intensive they are to code.

Experiment throughput is modeled as a function of experiment compute Cxpm⁢(t) (i.e. the H100-equivalents used to run research experiments) and coding labor LC⁢(t) (i.e. the number of full-time-human-equivalent coders). Although experiment throughput goes up with both experiment compute Cxpm⁢(t) and coding labor LC⁢(t), there are diminishing returns to only increasing one of the inputs and not the other. One way to model this relationship would be to use a Cobb-Douglas function: X⁢(t)=Cxpm⁢(t)α⁢LC⁢(t)β where 0<α<1 and 0<β<1. This has the property that if one of Cxpm⁢(t) or LC⁢(t) becomes much larger than the other, then increases in X⁢(t) come mainly from the smaller input, because the larger input exhibits diminishing returns.

One problem with the Cobb-Douglas function is that experiment throughput can go to infinity when one of Cxpm⁢(t) or LC⁢(t) goes to infinity but the other remains bounded, which isn’t realistic. If you have infinite coding labor but only a fixed amount of experiment compute, you can only implement so many experiments per unit time; and if you have infinite experiment compute but only a fixed number of coders, you don’t have enough coders to implement experiments that usefully use all the compute. (Technically, with infinite experiment compute you could search the space of all possible AI designs, but that takes time and thus does not provide an infinite speedup; further, we aren’t in that regime anyway.) In economic terms, we would say that experiment compute and coding labor are complementary inputs that cannot fully substitute for each other. This is often modeled with a CES (“constant elasticity of substitution”) production function with substitution parameter ρX<0, and we use this approach. See the following expandable box for mathematical details.

More details.

Experiment throughput is given by the following CES production function:

X⁢(t)=(α⁢C~xpm⁢(t)ρX+(1−α)⁢L~C⁢(t)ρX)1/ρX,0<α<1,ρX<0,

where

C~xpm⁢(t)=Cxpm⁢(t)ζ,L~C⁢(t)=LC⁢(t)λ,0<ζ<1, 0<λ<1.

What is λ? We use the smaller quantity LC⁢(t)λ instead of LC⁢(t) since LC⁢(t) is a measure of parallel coding labor but we want a measure of serial coding labor (a single programmer can make more progress in two days than a team of two programmers can make in a day because of the “stepping on toes” effect). The reason we use serial (rather than parallel) coding labor in the CES function is that if the project had unlimited experiment compute, the way we double experiment throughput is by letting the coding workforce work for twice as long (or speeding up the coding workforce by 2×), rather than doubling the size of the coding workforce. We estimated λ based on our intuitions about the effect of increased parallel labor on research effort, as well as interviews with frontier AI researchers. Our default value is λ=0.5, meaning that speeding up the coding workforce by 2× (i.e. doubling the serial coding workforce) is equivalent to a 4× increase of the number of coders (i.e. a 4× increase of the parallel coding workforce).

What is α? The closer 0<α<1 is to 1, the more experiment compute is considered important relative to coding labor.

What is ρX? The smaller ρX<0 is, the less it’s possible to only increase one of Cxpm⁢(t) and LC⁢(t) in order to increase X⁢(t). The CES expression has the property that: if we fix Cxpm⁢(t) and take LC⁢(t) to infinity, then X⁢(t) will asymptote to α1/ρ⁢C~xpm⁢(t); and if we fix LC⁢(t) and take Cxpm⁢(t) to infinity, then X⁢(t) will asymptote to (1−α)1/ρ⁢L~C⁢(t). In simple terms, when ρX<0 is small, the maximum possible boost you can get from increasing one of experiment compute or coding labor to infinity is lower.

What is ζ? We need to discount experiment compute Cxpm by the exponent 0<ζ<1 in order for our current functional form to give reasonable results. This is a bit strange since, given unlimited coding labor, it does seem like doubling experiment compute should let you run twice as many experiments. Here are two ways of understanding this:

  1. 1.

    doubling experiment throughput with unlimited coding labor may correspond to doubling the serial speed of the chips, rather than the (parallel) number of chips; and

  2. 2.

    as the size of models increases, the amount of compute required to run experiments on near-frontier-sized models increases.

Davidson also discounts experiment compute by an exponent ζ in his Full Takeoff Model; see the “production box” of his model description.

Setting the values of ζ,α,ρX: We estimated three quantities, from a fixed starting point of the present-day, that together pin down the values of ζ, α, and ρX. Namely:

  1. 1.

    how much smaller X⁢(present-day) would be if Cxpm⁢(present-day) were 10× smaller;

  2. 2.

    what X⁢(t) asymptotes to if we take Cxpm to infinity; and

  3. 3.

    what X⁢(t) asymptotes to if we take LC to infinity.

To estimate these quantities, we combined frontier AI researcher interviews, surveys of other AI experts, and our own reasoning about how X⁢(t) would change given infinite coding labor or compute. Our default values are ζ=0.65, α=0.81, and ρ=−0.155. See here for more details.

2.3 Research effort and AI R&D uplift

Research effort R⁢E⁢(t) is the number of quality-adjusted experiments the project can perform per unit time. That is, it’s a version of experiment throughput X⁢(t) but where experiments proposed by more skillful researchers are weighted more (experiment throughput only weighted experiments by the amount of experiment compute they required and how much coding labor they required to be implemented).

We model research effort R⁢E⁢(t) as the product of experiment throughput X⁢(t) and aggregate research taste T⁢(t):

R⁢E⁢(t)=T⁢(t)⁢X⁢(t).

Intuitively, T⁢(t) measures the average value per experiment. For example, if researcher A has 2× the research taste of researcher B, this means an experiment proposed by A is as valuable as two experiments proposed by B (assuming all three of these experiments are compute-equivalent and coding-labor-equivalent). For how research taste is calculated, see Aggregate Research Taste.

Related to research effort, we track AI software R&D uplift for each time t (called “AI software R&D progress multiplier” in our previous work). This concept is what it sounds like: it measures “how much faster R&D goes with the AIs compared with without the AIs”. Write R⁢E⁢(AIt→present-day) for what research effort would be in the present-day if we magically transported the AIs from time t back to the present-day. Then

AI software R&D uplift=R⁢E⁢(AIt→present-day)R⁢E⁢(present-day).

Note that in the definition of AI software R&D uplift, we only transport the AIs back to the present-day, not the extra experiment/automation compute and the extra human labor that will be available at time t.

2.4 Software efficiency

Recall that software efficiency S⁢(t) measures how efficiently the training process at time t can convert training compute into performance. We have S⁢(Feb 2025)=1, and S⁢(June 2027)=1000 would mean: if we used the training process of the Feb 2025, it would take 1000× more compute than in June 2027 to train models as performant as the best from June 2027.

We want to understand how S⁢(t) changes as research effort R⁢E⁢(t) changes. Technically though, S is not a function of R⁢E (which is a measure of “how much research is currently going on”), but of how much total work has cumulatively been done so far. So define research stock by:

R⁢S⁢(t)=total amount of research effort done by time t.

So research stock is to research effort what distance traveled is to speed. (In symbols, R⁢S⁢(t)=∫−∞tR⁢E⁢(τ)⁢𝑑τ.)

Both research stock and software efficiency describe “how far” the AI project has come, but research stock (like research effort) is about effort/investment while software efficiency is about fruits/returns. We want to know their relationship:

R⁢S≈ total AI software R&D work done→relationship?S≈ total returns

When we look at past empirical data, we see that S⁢(t) has been growing exponentially over time. Meanwhile research effort and research stock should also be growing exponentially, since both experiment compute and AI software R&D labor have been growing exponentially over the last 10 years. So, since S⁢(t) and R⁢S⁢(t) both grow exponentially over time (with different bases for the exponentials), the relationship between them is best modeled by a power law:

S⁢(t)=C⋅R⁢S⁢(t)1/β,for some fixed parameters C and β>0. (1)

Intuitively:

β doublings of research stock R⁢S→produces1 doubling of software efficiency S
How do we set the parameter β?

β is set based on other parameters rather than direct estimation. In short, we set β to ensure that the rate of software progress in 2024 equals a user-specified parameter.

We set β by:

  1. 1.

    Assuming a software efficiency growth rate in 2024 (see here for more on how we set the 2024 software efficiency growth rate).

  2. 2.

    Assuming that AI agents have provided no uplift to engineering labor or research taste between the start year (2012) and 2024 (this is why we chose to use 2024 rather than present day).

This makes research effort a pure function of time: you just look up the values of the exogenous compute and labor inputs and plug them into the production functions. From this you can calculate the growth rate of the research stock (cumulative research effort) in 2024.

Then, in accordance with (1), we set β to be the growth rate of research stock R⁢S in 2024 divided by the growth rate of software efficiency S in 2024. That is:

β=gR⁢S⁢(2024)gS⁢(2024).

Here gS⁢(t)=dd⁢t⁢ln⁡S⁢(t) and gR⁢S=dd⁢t⁢ln⁡R⁢S⁢(t).

Our model captures diminishing returns.

AI capabilities come from a combination of software efficiency S and training compute Ctrain. Since training compute has been growing exponentially, for S to “keep up” and not fade into irrelevance, it also needs to be growing exponentially. So it’s natural to talk about returns on research effort in terms of log⁡S (i.e. orders of magnitude of S). Since

log⁡S=log⁡C+1β⋅log⁡R⁢S≈1β⋅log⁡R⁢S,

successive equal-sized increases in R⁢S lead to smaller and smaller increases in log⁡S over time. This is the phenomenon of “diminishing returns”: the more progress has already been made, the harder it becomes to make impactful new discoveries since the low-hanging fruits have been picked.

Connection to the Jones semi-endogenous growth model.

Jones 1995 uses the following growth law to model idea production:

A′⁢(t)=a⁢A⁢(t)1−β⁢R⁢(t)λ.

Here A⁢(t) is the stock of ideas and R⁢(t) is the number of researchers engaged in idea production. The parameter λ>0 controls the “stepping on toes” effect from adding additional researchers, and β>0 represents the strength of the “fishing out” effect, according to which ideas become harder to find as the stock grows.

In past AGI takeoff models (e.g., Davidson’s FTM model and Epoch’s GATE model), software efficiency S⁢(t) replaced the stock of ideas A⁢(t). All proposed models have had the form

for various definitions of research effort R⁢E⁢(t).

3 Coding automation

We now turn to describing how our model sets AIs’ R&D capabilities and how that affects the inputs to software research effort.

3.1 Coding automation fraction

We think coding is reasonably well described as a stable set of types of tasks, and that some tasks will be (or already are) automatable far before others. We define the coding automation fraction A⁢(t) to be the fraction of coding work (involved in frontier AI research) that can be efficiently automated. For example, A⁢(March 2029)=80% means that we could use the AIs of March 2029 to automate away 80% of the coding work done in the present-day and not lose in productivity (80% as measured by the proportion of clone-parity coders doing that work; see the expandable box below).

When is a task considered efficiently automatable?

We define a “clone-parity coder” by the condition that, if every human coder were replaced by a clone-parity coder, productivity would remain the same. For each type of coding task, we weight its importance by how many clone-parity coders would work on the task absent AI assistance. We consider a task efficiently automatable when AIs can complete the task faster than the human coders and using no more than BN of present-day automation compute, where B is the number of human clone-parity-coder-equivalents working on the task in the present-day and N is the total number of human coders in the present-day.

Our definition of the Automated Coder milestone does not technically imply that A⁢(tAC)=1 at the time tAC when the milestone is reached. Nevertheless, we will make the simplifying assumption that this is the case.

Why doesn’t the definition of AC literally imply all tasks are efficiently automatable?

The Automated Coder (AC) milestone was defined by the condition that if the AC was dropped into the present day, it would overall be as productive as all present-day human coders with no AI assistance. This can be true even if it’s still less efficient than humans for some proportion of tasks.

We model coding automation fraction A⁢(t) as an S-shaped logistic function of OOMs (orders of magnitude) of effective compute log⁡E⁢(t). That is, we estimate the automation fraction A⁢(present-day) in the present, and let A⁢(t) vary logistically as a function of log⁡E⁢(t) until A⁢(t) reaches 1 when log⁡E⁢(t) equals the effective compute log⁡EAC for the Automated Coder anchor. (In order to make the logistic function actually attain 1, we choose the right asymptote of the logistic to be greater than 1, but then truncate when we reach a coding automation fraction of 1.)

We set the present-day coding automation fraction A⁢(present-day) so that the model’s prediction of the coding uplift in the present-day agrees with the results of surveys of people at AGI companies. Our median value for the coding uplift of present-day AIs at AGI companies is that having the AIs is like having 1.8 times as many software engineers (and all the staff necessary to coordinate them effectively). See here for more details on these coding uplift surveys.

3.2 Coding automation efficiency

We use indices i in the interval [0,1] to represent the various coding tasks involved in implementing research experiments. At any given time t, tasks i such that 0≤i≤A⁢(t) are considered efficiently automatable, while tasks i such that A⁢(t)<i≤1 are not.

For each task i, the coding automation efficiency ηi⁢(t) is the conversion rate between units of automation compute (i.e. H100-equivalents) and the equivalent in human coders. In other words, if Caut,i⁢(t) is the amount of compute assigned to task i, then this corresponds to having ηi⁢(t)⁢Caut,i⁢(t) additional human coders work on task i. So ηi⁢(t) is a measure of the runtime efficiency of AIs for task i; efficiency gains come from a combination of making AIs faster, being able to run more AIs, and getting more capable AIs that can complete tasks with fewer actions.

Writing Ei for the effective compute corresponding to when task i is first efficiently automatable, our model for ηi⁢(t) is:

ηi⁢(t)={ηinit⋅(E⁢(t)Ei)ηslopeif task i is efficiently automatable by time t;0otherwise;

where ηinit is a parameter representing the initial efficiency when a task first gets automatable, and ηslope is a parameter controlling how quickly automated coders’ efficiency continues to improve afterward. This can be intuitively understood as:

each OOM of effective compute→givesηslope OOMs of coding automation efficiency.

By our definition of when a coding task is efficiently automatable, the initial conversion rate ηinit is the ratio of the total number of human coders to the amount of automation compute in the present-day.

This model of automation efficiency ηi⁢(t) makes the simplifying assumption that the relationship between effective compute and efficiency stays consistent across different efficiency levels, which is technically false. However, we think changing this would not substantially affect results. (Our model also imposes a cap on the maximum coding automation efficiency you can achieve with a fixed automation compute budget. This doesn’t really affect the model’s outputs, however, since way before reaching these caps, the AGI project’s ability to run experiments will bottleneck on experiment compute, not coding labor.)

The coding automation efficiency slope parameter ηslope was estimated by looking at data about how quickly AIs’ inference cost and speed has been improving; see here for more details.

3.3 Aggregate coding labor

We next discuss how the increasing AI coding abilities feed back into aggregate coding labor, which in turn influences experiment throughput. Recall that experiment throughput (i.e. the capacity of the project to implement and run experiments) was a function of coding labor and experiment compute, and coding labor LC⁢(t) was described as “the number of full-time-human-equivalents”. We want LC⁢(t) to account for both human coding labor and AI coding labor, so we now call it “aggregate coding labor”. We write LC,H⁢(t) for the number of human coders (LC,H⁢(t) is an exogenous time series we set based on extrapolating past data of AGI company researcher/engineer workforce growth; see here for details).

Recall that we use indices i in the interval [0,1] to represent the different coding tasks involved in implementing AI experiments. We write LC,H,i⁢(t) and Caut,i⁢(t), respectively, for the amount of human coding labor and AI coding labor allocated to task i. The aggregate coding labor for task i is

Gi⁢(t)=LC,H,i⁢(t)+ηi⁢(t)⋅Caut,i⁢(t),

since ηi⁢(t) is the conversion rate between units of automation compute and human coders for task i.

If AIs cheaply automate some coding tasks but not others, what effects will that have on coding labor? This depends how much one can substitute the automatable tasks for the non-automatable tasks. But if Gi⁢(t) goes to infinity for, say, 90% of the tasks, this shouldn’t cause LC⁢(t) to go to infinity since coding will then bottleneck on the remaining 10% of tasks. We choose to model this using a task-based CES (“constant elasticity of substitution”) production function with a parameter ρC<0 that controls the level of substitutability. We are unsure about how well task-based CES functions model reality, and there are known issues with them (see this paper for example), but they are the best tool we know of for this job. We’d be interested in work that more granularly models the coding workflow.

Explicit formula for LC⁢(t).
LC⁢(t)=(∫01Gi⁢(t)ρC⁢𝑑i)1/ρC=(∫01(LC,H,i⁢(t)+ηi⁢(t)⋅Caut,i⁢(t))ρC⁢𝑑i)1/ρC.

What is ρC? The smaller the substitution parameter ρC<0, the more coding bottlenecks on the tasks with little coding labor. Put differently, ρC much smaller than 0 would mean coding AI experiments is a fairly rigid set of tasks that must always be done, and ρC close to 0 would mean that one can more easily substitute easy-to-automate tasks for hard-to-automate tasks. See here for details on how we set ρC.

When running simulations of our model, at each timestep we set the allocations LC,H,i⁢(t) and Caut,i⁢(t) to maximize the aggregate coding labor LC⁢(t) (subject to the constraints that the allocations LC,H,i sum to the total human coding labor LC,H⁢(t) and the allocations Caut,i⁢(t) sum to the total automation compute Caut⁢(t)). This corresponds to the AI project allocating its human and AI coding workforce to where it’s most useful; more concretely, the human coders will be reallocated to tasks that still evade automation while the AI coders will be allocated to the tasks they can efficiently automate (especially those tasks they can automate very quickly).

4 Research taste automation

The research taste of a human or AI is meant to encompass various things, e.g., high-level research direction setting, low-level choices, and experiment ideation and interpretation. In our model, research taste is basically operationalized as the value one gets per experiment. Doubling research taste would have the same effect on progress as doubling experiment throughput (that is, as doubling the number of experiments the project can implement per unit time).

In this section we explain our model of how AIs’ research taste improves with effective compute and how the research tastes of humans and AIs combine to give the aggregate research taste.

We discussed how Stage 1 of our model between the present-day and the Automated Coder milestone is largely driven by extrapolating the METR time horizon trend (and deciding whether to extrapolate with an exponential, a superexponential, or a subexponential). Stage 2 of the model behavior, once the Automated Coder milestone is reached, is largely driven by how quickly the research taste of the AIs increases with effective compute, as well as the AIs’ initial research taste at the Automated Coder milestone. The complete automation (and speedup) of coding also plays a notable role, but based on our model simulations the rate at which AIs improve at research taste is a bigger driver. Intuitively, additional coding labor can only help so much because the ability to run experiments will eventually bottleneck on experiment compute. On the other hand, it’s always helpful to have more skillful automated selection of experiments.

4.1 Human research taste

Before we get to improvements in automated research taste (i.e. AIs’ research taste), we should discuss our model of human research taste. We model the distribution of research taste of human researchers (at a leading AGI company) with a lognormal distribution TH of median 1 (“lognormal” means log⁡(TH) is a normal distribution). To choose the variance of the lognormal, we first estimated the ratio Tspread between the research taste of the top current researcher and that of the current median researcher at a leading AGI company, via surveys of frontier AI researchers (this gives a median of 3.7; see here for more details on how we set this top-to-median taste parameter). Assuming an AGI company today has 1000 human researchers, this leads us to choose the lognormal TH to have a ratio of Tspread between the 99.9th percentile value of TH and the median value of TH. (Later we will use that the 99.9th percentile value of TH corresponds to three standard deviations above the median of the underlying normal distribution.)

Why use a lognormal distribution for human research taste?

We choose a lognormal distribution because based on surveys of and discussions with frontier AI researchers, it seems likely that the top few percent of researchers provide much more value via research taste than typical researchers. Additionally, analogous quantities like scientific citation counts or startup success are often empirically lognormal.

4.2 Automated research taste

As for coding automation fraction and efficiency, the automated research taste TA⁢I⁢(t) is modeled as a function of effective compute. Whereas we represent the human research taste by a lognormal distribution TH∼Lognormal⁢(0,σ2), TA⁢I⁢(t) is a single number for each time t (we think of the AIs as a single collective entity, unlike for humans).

We describe the AIs’ research taste in terms of how many standard deviations σ (of the underlying normal of the human distribution) above or below the human median it is, and write TA⁢IS⁢D⁢(t) for this quantity. If TA⁢IS⁢D⁢(t) is negative, this means the AIs’ research taste is below the human median; and if TA⁢IS⁢D⁢(t) is positive, this means the AIs’ research taste is above the human median.

We assume the AIs’ research taste increases by Trate standard deviations for each additional OOM (order of magnitude) of effective compute:

TA⁢IS⁢D⁢(t)=TACS⁢D+Trate⁢(log⁡E⁢(t)−log⁡EAC)in human standard deviations σ. (2)

Here TACS⁢D is a parameter for the research taste of the Automated Coder (in standard deviations), and EAC is the effective compute corresponding to the Automated Coder (EAC is determined by the part of the model described in Time Horizon and the Automated Coder Milestone). The parameter TACS⁢D was set based on surveys of frontier AI researchers and AI experts, plus a minor intuitive adjustment; Eli’s median is TACS⁢D=0.5 (meaning the AC would be slightly better than the median human researcher) while Daniel’s median is TAS⁢D=0 (meaning the AC would be at the level of the median human researcher); see here for more details.

To set the research taste slope Trate, we looked at data about how quickly AIs have moved through the human range for a variety of tasks. We etsimate Trate as a function of the growth rate of effective compute in present day, gE⁢(present-day), as we can more confidently assess the release date of models in our data than their effective compute. Eli and Daniel’s medians are Trate=2.1gE⁢(present-day) and Trate=3gE⁢(present-day), respectively. See here for more details on setting Trate.

4.2.1 Near the human range

While the AIs’ research taste is near the human range, the AIs’ research taste TA⁢I⁢(t) (in absolute units now, not standard deviations) is approximately obtained by converting from the normal distribution back to the lognormal distribution. That is, TA⁢I⁢(t)≈eσ⁢TA⁢IS⁢D⁢(t) when the AIs are near the human range; and so by our expression (2) for TA⁢IS⁢D, each additional OOM of effective compute means multiplying the AIs’ research taste by approximately eTrate⁢σ.

In Human Research Taste, we assumed that the 99.9th percentile human researcher (i.e. the best human researcher in a population of 1000) has research taste Tspread times greater than the median human researcher. This 99.9th percentile researcher corresponds to (roughly) 3 standard deviations above the median (since roughly 99.9% of the area under a normal distribution bell curve is to the left of 3 standard deviations above the median; e.g. see the bell curve picture above). This all means that e3⁢σ=Tspread. So, near the human range, we have:

TA⁢IS⁢D increases by 1→meansTA⁢I is multiplied by eσ=(Tspread)1/3.

Combining with (2), near the human range we have:

1 OOM of effective compute→meansTA⁢I is multiplied by (Tspread)Trate/3.

4.2.2 Our more general model of TA⁢I addressing algorithmic limits

Our actual model for TA⁢I⁢(t) is a bit more complicated, because we want to incorporate an upper bound for how high research taste can get. So while initially each increment of TA⁢IS⁢D by 1 results in a multiplier of roughly (Tspread)Trate/3, eventually TA⁢I⁢(t) plateaus when TA⁢IS⁢D gets very large. We also think the rate of progress in research taste should slow down gradually, rather than abruptly, as we approach the upper limit.

So, instead of TA⁢I=eσ⁢TA⁢IS⁢D, we use a kind of generalized logistic function as our model of TA⁢I as a function of TA⁢IS⁢D. It’s illustrated in the following graph (the orange dotted line is explained later):

Near the human range (i.e. when TA⁢IS⁢D is near 0), TA⁢I is approximately equal to eσ⁢TA⁢IS⁢D=(Tspread)TA⁢IS⁢D/3. We also have a parameter 0<L<1 (called the “research taste slowdown factor halfway to algorithmic limits”) which controls how quickly TA⁢I approaches the upper limit A as a function of TA⁢IS⁢D. L is interpreted as follows: at the point when TA⁢I is halfway between 1 and A in log space (i.e. when TA⁢I=A1/2), increasing TA⁢IS⁢D by 1 means multiplying TA⁢I by eL⁢σ=(Tspread)L/3 (so the growth rate at the halfway point (in log scale) is L times what it was in the human range). The bigger L is, the steeper the orange dotted line is, and the faster TA⁢I approaches the limit A. The graph uses Eli’s default values of A=130,000 and L=0.5.

Setting the values of the parameters A and L.

To set A, we looked at data about how superhuman chess and Go AIs are, and made some minor adjustments based on how these activities differ from machine learning research. We thought of A in terms of the number of top-to-median-human gaps above the best human that would correspond to the limits of research taste. Eli’s median is 8 top-to-median-human gaps, so that A=Tspread⋅Tspread8=3.7⋅3.78≈130,000. On the other hand, Daniel’s median is 16 top-to-median-human gaps. Obviously there’s a lot of uncertainty about research taste limits. See here for more discussion.

Here’s Eli’s explanation for choosing a median value of L=0.5: “0.5 is chosen because it seems intuitively roughly right and also leads to behavior that seems reasonable to me, along with my median for maximum AI research taste. In particular, it leads to slowing down software-only takeoffs from SAR → ASI by about 1.5-3x, with the slowdown being larger if the takeoff is faster. That said, I have a lot of uncertainty as it seems hard to get good traction on estimating the parameter. So I specify a beta with 5th percentile ∼0.13 and 95th percentile ∼0.87.”

Mathematical details about expressing TA⁢I as a function of TA⁢IS⁢D.

Our model for TA⁢I is

TA⁢I=(Aρ+(1−Aρ)⁢eρ⁢v⁢TA⁢IS⁢D)1/ρ.

As above, A is the research taste upper bound. The parameter ρ (which can be positive or negative) is related to the parameter L mentioned earlier, and controls how quickly the upper bound of A is reached; explicitly (see the derivation below), we have

ρ=2⁢ln⁡(1L−1)ln⁡A. (3)

Finally,

When ρ<0, i.e. L>0.5, we can rearrange TA⁢I⁢(t) into a more interpretable logistic-sigmoid form:

TA⁢I=A(1+C⁢e−D⁢TA⁢IS⁢D)B, (5)

More on interpreting TA⁢IS⁢D, and the definitions of SIAR, TED-AI, and ASI.

Because we impose an upper limit on research taste, the human distribution of research taste is not literally lognormal (since a lognormal is unbounded). For example, even if the size of the human research population was increased by many orders of magnitude, the research taste of the best human researcher would not be as high as what the lognormal distribution predicts. Instead, we think a better way to conceptually model the human distribution of research taste may be to use TH=TA⁢I⁢(𝒩⁢(0;1)), so that P⁢(TH≤y)=P⁢(𝒩⁢(0;1)≤TA⁢I−1⁢(y)) (=Φ⁢(TA⁢I−1⁢(y)) where Φ is the CDF of the standard normal). Put differently, if we look at the TA⁢I vs TA⁢IS⁢D figure, P⁢(TH≤y) is obtained by finding the TA⁢IS⁢D value corresponding to that value of y, then noting that P⁢(TH≤y) equals the probability the standard normal is at most that value of TA⁢IS⁢D.

Using this model of human distribution of research taste, we interpret an AI having (SD-)research taste TA⁢IS⁢D=x to mean that the AI is as good as the P⁢(𝒩⁢(0;1)≤x)-th (i.e. Φ⁢(x)-th) percentile human researcher. Talking about the 99.999999th percentile human researcher is a bit strange since the best current human researcher (in our model, which assumes a population of 1,000 researchers) is already 99.99th percentile. So the 99.999999th percentile human researcher would be relative to a hypothetical situation where we increased the size of the human resesarcher population by several orders of magnitude.

This interpretation of TA⁢IS⁢D is used when defining a SIAR (Superintelligent AI Researcher). We defined a SIAR by saying that the gap between a SIAR and the top AGI project human researcher is 2x greater than the gap between the top AGI project human researcher and the median researcher. Since the top-to-median-human gap corresponds to 3 standard deviations, our precise definition of SIAR is an AI whose S⁢D-research taste is TA⁢IS⁢D=9=3+2×3. So, in our model, this would map to an absolute research taste of TA⁢I⁢(9) and correspond to the research taste of the Φ⁢(9)th percentile human researcher (and Φ⁢(9)=1−1.27×10−19!). To reiterate, for us the level of a SIAR is defined relative to humans (concretely what research taste percentile the SIAR corresponds to for a hypothetically much larger human researcher population), rather than relative to some more intrinsic metric. Similar comments hold for our definition of ASI, though ASI is defined with respect to cognitive abilities other than research taste for AI R&D. See here for more.

Because our model does not directly model cognitive abilities other than AI R&D, we operationalize TED-AI and ASI in terms of guesses for the number of top-to-median-human research taste SD-gaps above the top human researcher needed to reach these milestones. For our default parameters:

  • •

    SAR corresponds to TA⁢IS⁢D=3 (i.e. SAR has the research taste of the best human researcher, which is 3 SDs above the median researcher).

  • •

    SIAR corresponds to TA⁢IS⁢D=9 (i.e. you need 2 top-to-median-human gaps above SAR to reach SIAR).

  • •

    TED-AI corresponds to TA⁢IS⁢D=7.5 (i.e. you need 1.5 top-to-median-human gaps above SAR to reach TED-AI).

  • •

    ASI corresponds to TA⁢IS⁢D=13.5 (i.e. you need 2 top-to-median-human gaps above TED-AI to read ASI).

4.3 Aggregate research taste

The aggregate research taste T⁢(t) measures the average value per experiment, when one accounts for both human and AI research taste. When the AIs’ research taste becomes better than that of the best human researcher, then T⁢(t) is just TA⁢I⁢(t). Before that’s the case, our model assumes that each human researcher with lower skill than the AIs is lifted up to the level of the AIs.

For each time t, we then define the aggregate research taste as the average of the research tastes of the human researchers uplifted by the AIs (recall that TH was a certain lognormal distribution):

T⁢(t)=𝔼⁢[max⁡(TH,TA⁢I⁢(t))]. (8)

For example, if TA⁢I⁢(July 2030)=2, then the aggregate research taste in July 2030 is 𝔼⁢[max⁡(TH,2)] (humans with research taste less than 2 are now thought to have research taste equal to 2, and humans with research taste greater than 2 are not affected). And if TA⁢I⁢(t) is larger than the research taste of the best human researcher for later t, then (8) reduces to TA⁢I⁢(t)=T⁢(t).

We currently do not model the quantity of labor doing research, and believe this would likely not qualitatively shift our results, since for research taste, quality matters much more than quantity. We instead build efficiency into our definition of research taste, allowing a fixed compute budget on which an AI’s research taste is evaluated. That said, this is an important limitation of our model, and we’re not confident in how big of an effect it would be to explicity model research labor quantity.

5 How does our model behave after full AI R&D automation?

In the introduction, we discussed how our model’s progression could be carved up into three natural stages. (Recall that AI software R&D uplift is just a constant multiple of research effort; see Research effort and AI R&D uplift.)

Stage 3 kicks off once the superhuman AI researcher (SAR) milestone is reached; AI software R&D is then fully automated.

Our model’s operationalization of SAR.

A SAR, if dropped into an AGI project in the present-day, would be as good at research taste as if there were only human researchers, who were each made as competent at research taste as the top researcher. The SAR must also qualify as an automated coder (AC), i.e. be able to fully automate coding in the AGI project.

The SAR’s compute budget for research taste is 1% of the project’s compute. As with an AC, it has a budget of 5% of the project’s compute for coding (coding has a higher compute budget because more human labor is currently spent on coding than experiment ideation/selection).

Our operationalization is similar to what we defined as a superhuman AI researcher (SAR) from our previous work, except that it only needs to match the productivity of the project’s best researchers, rather than the project’s best researchers sped up by 30x and 30x more numerous. We also separate research taste from coding.

At this point, there are two qualitatively different possibilities:

  1. 1.

    There is a taste-only singularity (TOS), meaning that doublings of AI software R&D uplift get faster and faster, even if compute remains unchanged and we ignore improvements in coding. In other words, the research-taste-driven AI R&D feedback loop (gains in research taste lead to gains in sofware AI R&D, which lead to gains in software efficiency, which lead to more gains in research taste) accelerates over time with fixed compute stock and fixed (aggregate) coding labor. In our model, this accelerated feedback loop continues until the AIs get closer to the algorithmic limits of research taste (the conversion rate of software efficiency into research taste smoothly decays over time, rather than abruptly stopping at the limit of research taste; see this previous section).

  2. 2.

    There is no taste-only singularity, because the rate at which valuable research ideas are getting harder to find outpaces improvements in research taste. On its own, the research taste feedback loop would fizzle. Takeoff speed is then determined by improvements in AIs’ coding abilities and the growth rate of compute (training, experiment, automation compute).

How does a “taste-only singularity” relate to a “software-only singularity”?

Others have referred to the possibility of a software-only singularity (SOS) or a software intelligence explosion (SIE) (most notably, in Forethought’s recent work here and here). In Forethought’s definition, there is an SIE if, after full AI R&D automation, doublings of software efficiency get faster over time without being dependent on increases in compute.

Their model considers cognitive labor as a single bulk quantity which combines with experiment compute to produce software research. The functional form they use allows for arbitrarily large levels of software research effort given sufficient cognitive labor. And, like our model, their model also expresses software efficiency as a power-law function of cumulative cognitive labor (i.e. research stock). Since more efficient algorithms allow running more automated researchers, a potentially explosive feedback loop results. The end behavior is governed by a single parameter r: the number of doublings of automated-researcher quantity that result from each doubling of cumulative cognitive labor.

Our model considers coding labor and research taste separately. The research effort multiplier from taking only coding labor to infinity, holding experiment compute and research taste constant, asymptotes at a relatively low value; a 15⁢x increase in the median case. On the other hand, the gain one gets from maximally capable research taste is (in most simulations) much higher; roughly a 10,000⁢x increase with Eli’s median parameter estimates. So both coding labor and research taste can contribute substantially to an intelligence explosion, but research taste has a higher ceiling and thus fizzles out less quickly.

The closest condition for which our model has a neat mathematical characterization is a taste-only singularity, or TOS. A TOS occurs when, if we assume training compute and experiment throughput are constant (i.e. constant compute and coding labor, leaving only research taste varying), doublings of AI R&D uplift get faster over time. For the purposes of this definition, we also assume that there are no limits to the value of research taste, but in practice the doubling times eventually get slower due to limits (which, as mentioned above, are usually high under our parameter estimates).

A taste-only singularity is a stricter condition than an SIE because it only allows for improvement on the taste dimension, and not coding or other skills. That said, we discuss it because we think that in practice taste will be the most important determinant of takeoff speeds; and indeed the extent to which we have a TOS is strongly correlated with takeoff speeds in our model. Not having a taste-only singularity does not necessarily mean that takeoff is slow; contributions from coding labor and compute increases still matter substantially (coding labor especially matters during early portions of takeoff). And if our model included hardware R&D automation and the industrial explosion, compute increases and coding labor would matter more strongly.

In this section, we explain when our model predicts a TOS. That is, we identify a simple condition on our model parameters that corresponds to a TOS (i.e. doublings of AI R&D uplift getting faster and faster over time, even with fixed compute and aggregate coding labor). We assume all types of compute (training compute, experiment compute, automation compute) are constant. In particular, gains in effective compute will only come from gains in software efficiency (not training compute). Since we’re also assuming aggregate coding labor is constant, experiment throughput (i.e. the number of experiments the AGI project can run) is constant. (Eventually experiment throughput will bottleneck on experiment compute with further coding improvements mattering little; but this can happen after the Superhuman AI Researcher milestone is achieved and so in practice coding improvements usually play a meaningful role in affecting the speed of takeoff. Our simple analysis of when there is a TOS leaves the effect of coding improvements aside.)

The conclusion is that our model predicts a TOS when

m>β,

where m=Trate⁢log⁡((Tspread)1/3) is the number of doublings of research taste you get for each doubling of software efficiency, and β is a parameter measuring how difficult it is to find impactful new software research ideas (see explanations following the next figure). Intuitively, m>β means that the rate of research-taste-driven improvements in AI software R&D uplift outpace the rate at which ideas get harder to find.

In fact, it turns out (see the expandable box below) that the ratio of successive doubling times for AI software R&D uplift is 2βm−1. For example, if we use Eli’s median parameters m=0.25 and β=0.315, then there is no TOS and each doubling of AI R&D uplift would take 20.3150.25−1≈1.2 times longer than the previous doubling of AI R&D uplift. A curious feature of how our model works is that each doubling time of R&D uplift can at most be twice as fast as the previous doubling (since 2βm−1 is always at least 2−1=1/2); this is already extremely fast but we think it’s probably a quirk of our model and in principle we could have takeoffs that are even faster in the real world.

Even if Eli’s median parameters don’t give a TOS, running Monte Carlo simulations that sample from Eli’s parameter distributions leads to a TOS happening in 38% of the simulations.

What is m again? In our model of Automated Research Taste, we assumed each new OOM of effective compute means the AIs’ research taste goes up by Trate standard deviations (of the normal distribution underlying the (approximately) lognormal distribution of human research taste). Tspread was the ratio of the research tastes between the best human researcher and the median human researcher and it corresponded to 3 standard deviations (the 3 in the exponent comes from rounding Φ−1⁢(0.999)≈3.09). All in all, our model of AIs’ research taste TA⁢I tells us that, at least when the AIs are far from algorithmic limits, each new OOM of effective compute results in multiplying TA⁢I by approximately

(Tspread)Trate/3=10Trate⁢log⁡((Tspread)1/3).

That is, each new OOM of effective compute increases TA⁢I by (approximately) m=Trate⁢log⁡((Tspread)1/3) OOMs. Equivalently, each doubling of effective compute results in (approximately) m=Trate⁢log⁡((Tspread)1/3) doublings of TA⁢I (the log is still base 10). If we’re assuming compute stays fairly constant, increases in effective compute come only from increases in software efficiency. Because we’re assuming that experiment throughput is constant (since experiment compute bottlenecks prevent the project from running more experiments), and also that aggregate research taste is just automated research taste (since human researchers are no longer adding value), gains in AIs’ research taste correspond to gains in research effort. Putting everything together, m satisfies

1 doubling of software efficiency S→givesm doublings of research effort R⁢E. (9)

What is β again? In Software Efficiency, we introduced research stock R⁢S⁢(t) as the total amount of research effort done by time t. Our model was:

β OOMs of research stock R⁢S→means1 OOM of software efficiency S. (10)

The bigger β is, the harder it is to discover impactful software research ideas (that will increase software efficiency). In our model, β is automatically set to ensure that the 2024 software efficiency growth rate equals a user-specified parameter (see Software Efficiency for more details).

Math details justifying the m>β condition for a TOS.

We want to identify when a TOS occurs, i.e. when doubling times of software efficiency are getting shorter and shorter (before progress slows down because of algorithmic limits, and assuming constant compute and aggregate coding labor). A clean way to do this is to use the semi-endogenous growth model formulation of the relationship between research effort R⁢E and software efficiency S (see the expandable box here):

S′⁢(t)=a⁢S⁢(t)1−β⁢R⁢E⁢(t),for some constant a>0. (11)

A useful way to measure the rate of change of S in OOMs is to look at the derivative of the logarithm of S (because the formula is easier, we use the natural logarithm ln). We call this the growth rate of S and write

gS⁢(t)=dd⁢t⁢ln⁡(S⁢(t))=S′⁢(t)S⁢(t).

Dividing both sides of (11) by S⁢(t) yields

gS⁢(t)=a⁢S⁢(t)−β⁢R⁢E⁢(t). (12)

There is a singularity if gS gets bigger over time (at least before progress slows due to algorithmic limits).

We want to express R⁢E⁢(t) in terms of S⁢(t). From our assumption that compute and aggregate coding labor are fixed, whenever S goes up by 1 OOM, R⁢E goes up by m OOMs. This is equivalent to saying that R⁢E and S satisfy a power law relationship:

R⁢E⁢(t)=C⁢S⁢(t)m,where C is a constant. (13)

Plugging this back into (12) gives

gS⁢(t)=a⁢C⁢S⁢(t)m−β=constant⋅S⁢(t)m−β. (14)

This expression shows that gS gets bigger with S (i.e. gS gets bigger over time since S increases over time) precisely when m>β. In other words, our model predicts a TOS precisely when m>β.

We can also use the equation (14) to study how successive doubling times change. The ratio of successive doubling times of S is given by

Doubling-Time⁢(2⁢S)Doubling-Time⁢(S) =gS⁢(S)gS⁢(2⁢S)
=constant⋅Sm−βconstant⋅(2⁢S)m−β
=2β−m,

where we wrote gS⁢(S) and gS⁢(2⁢S) for the growth rates of S at the beginning of the first and second doubling periods of S, respectively. So each doubling time of S is 2β−m as long as the previous doubling time, at least initially when the AIs are far from algorithmic limits.

We can also find the ratio of successive doubling times for research effort R⁢E, or equivalently for AI R&D uplift (recall that AI R&D uplift is just a constant multiple of research effort; see the end of Research Effort and AI R&D uplift). By (13), doublings of R⁢E correspond to 21/mx-ings of S. Just as we calculated the ratio of successive doubling times of S to be 2β−m, we can use the same method to calculate the ratio of successive 21/mx-ings of S (there’s nothing privileged about doublings or OOMs or using any other particular base for the logarithmic scale). This gives

ratio of successive 21/mx-ings of S=(21/m)β−m=2βm−1.

Since doublings of AI R&D uplift are the same as doublings of R⁢E, which are the same as 21/mx-ings of S, we conclude:

ratio of successive doublings of AI R&D uplift=2βm−1.

So each doubling time of AI R&D uplift is 2βm−1 as long as the previous doubling time, at least initially when the AIs are far from algorithmic limits.

The default values mentioned are Eli’s median parameters.

6.1 Input time series

  • •

    Human coding labor LC,H⁢(t).

  • •

    Compute, which is allocated as fixed percentages between training compute Ctrain (8%), experiment compute Cxpm (50%), and automation compute Caut (5%). (The remaining compute is allocated to external inference.)

  • •

    See our supplementary materials for more details on how we set these input time series.

6.2 Time horizon modeling

  • •

    Time horizon H⁢(t) is a function of log(effective compute) =log⁡E. Starting from H⁢(present-day), the first time horizon doubling requires log⁡E to increase by τ, the second doubling by d⁢τ, the third doubling by d2⁢τ, and so forth. Provided H⁢(t) is not infinite yet (which eventually happens for d<1):

    H⁢(t)={H⁢(present-day)⋅2log⁡E⁢(t)−log⁡E⁢(present-day)τif d=1;H⁢(present-day)⋅(1−(1−d)⁢log⁡E⁢(t)−log⁡E⁢(present-day)τ)logd⁡2if d≠1.

    The default value for d is 0.92; in particular, H is then a superexponential function of log⁡E.

  • •

    The Automated Coder milestone is reached when H⁢(t) equals a parameter HAC. The default value for HAC is 130 work-years.

6.3 Effective compute modeling

  • •

    Effective compute

    E⁢(t)=Ctrain⁢(t)⁢S⁢(t),

    is the amount of training compute needed to train the frontier AIs at time t given the training algorithms of the present-day. All other capability metrics (time horizon, coding automation fraction/efficiency, research taste) are expressed in terms of effective compute.

  • •

    Experiment throughput X⁢(t) is a CES of experiment compute and serial coding labor:

    X⁢(t)=(α⁢C~xpm⁢(t)ρX+(1−α)⁢L~C⁢(t)ρX)1/ρX,0<α<1,ρX<0.
    • –

      L~C⁢(t)=LC⁢(t)λ, where the default parallelization penalty is λ=0.5.

    • –

      C~exp⁢(t)=Cexp⁢(t)ζ.

    • –

      The parameters ζ,α,ρX are pinned down by specifying parameters for (a) how much smaller X⁢(present-day) would be if Cxpm⁢(present-day) were 10x smaller, (b) what X⁢(present-day) would be if Cxpm were infinity, (c) what X⁢(present-day) would be if LC were infinity. The default values are ζ=0.65, α=0.81, and ρX=−0.155. See here for details.

  • •

    Research effort is

    R⁢E⁢(t)=X⁢(t)⁢T⁢(t),

    where T⁢(t) is aggregate research taste.

  • •

    AI software R&D uplift is

    R⁢E⁢(A⁢It→present-day)/R⁢E⁢(present-day),

    where R⁢E⁢(A⁢It→present-day) is what research effort would be in the present-day if we transported the AIs at time t back to the present-day (but not the extra experiment/automation compute and the extra human labor available at time t).

  • •

    Software efficiency S⁢(t) follows the Jones semi-endogenous growth model

    S′⁢(t)=S⁢(t)1−β⁢R⁢E⁢(t).

    The parameter β determines how much harder software progress gets when S gets large: the cumulated research effort (“research stock”) needs to double β times for S to double.

6.4 Coding automation modeling

  • •

    The percentage of automatable coding tasks is a logistic function of log⁡E. In our model, this percentage equals 1 at the effective compute corresponding to AC (this is a simplification since by definition an AC is attained when its coding productivity matches that of all human coders in the present-day, and this may happen before all tasks are automated). The percentage of automatable coding tasks in the present-day (33% in Dec 2025 for default parameters) is chosen so that the model correctly predicts the coding uplift in the present-day (a parameter based on surveys of people at AI companies).

  • •

    Coding tasks are indexed by 0≤i≤1.

  • •

    Once a task i gets automated, it will get more efficient over time by a multiplier ηi⁢(t).

    • –

      ηi⁢(t) is 0 before task i is automated;

    • –

      When effective compute reaches the level Ei required to automate task i, ηi increases as a power law of effective compute:

      ηi⁢(t)=ηinit⁢(E⁢(t)Ei)ηslope.
    • –

      The initial efficiency ηinit equals the ratio of the number of human coders to the amount of automation compute in the present-day, and ηslope is estimated via data about how quickly inference cost and speed has been improving.

  • •

    Overall coding labor for task i is

    Gi⁢(t)=LC,H,i⁢(t)+ηi⁢Caut,i,

    where LC,H,i⁢(t) is the number of human coders on task i and Caut,i⁢(t) is the compute used to run AIs working on task i.

  • •

    Aggregate coding labor LC⁢(t) is a CES between the labor applied to all different tasks, with low substitutability ρC=−2 by default:

    LC⁢(t)=(∫01Gi⁢(t)ρC⁢𝑑i)1/ρC=(∫01(LC,H,i⁢(t)+ηi⁢(t)⁢Caut,i⁢(t))Cρ⁢𝑑i)1/ρC.
  • •

    Human coding labor LC,H,i⁢(t) and automation compute Caut,i are allocated between tasks i to maximize LC⁢(t) at each time step.

6.5 Research taste automation

  • •

    Human research taste TH is lognormally distributed, with the median human researcher having taste 1 and the 99.9th percentile (+3.1 SDs) human researcher considered the best researcher. The default value for the research taste of the current best human researchers is 3.7x (this was informed by surveys of AI researchers and experts).

  • •

    By default, an AC has research taste TACS⁢D=0.5 SDs above the median human researcher (parameter based on surveys of AI researchers and experts).

  • •

    In terms of SDs, automated research taste is

    TA⁢IS⁢D⁢(t)=TACS⁢D+Trate⁢(log⁡E−log⁡EAC).
  • •

    Actual automated resesarch taste is a generalized logistic of TA⁢IS⁢D:

    TA⁢I⁢(t)=(Aρ+(1−Aρ)⁢eρ⁢v⁢TA⁢IS⁢D)1/ρ.
    • –

      A is the ultimate limit of research taste;

    • –

      ρ controls how quickly the limit A is reached;

    • –

  • •

Aggregate research taste is the average of the research tastes of the human researchers after having been (possibly) uplifted:

    T⁢(t)=𝔼⁢[max⁡(TH,TA⁢I⁢(t))].
